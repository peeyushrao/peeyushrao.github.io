### SOC decisions should be risk-driven, not severity-driven.

SOC decisions must be risk-driven because risk integrates the technical
severity of an alert with business context, asset criticality, and the
actual likelihood of an adversary succeeding. While severity is a
point-in-time score assigned by a security tool to an event, risk is the
calculation of a threat\'s potential impact against the probability of
that threat exploiting a specific vulnerability.

**The Limitations of Severity-Driven Prioritisation\**

Relying solely on tool-defined severity can lead to significant
operational inefficiencies:

> • Alert Fatigue: Tool-based severity scores often generate a high
> volume of \"high-priority\" noise that may not be relevant to the
> organisation\'s specific environment, leading to analyst burnout and
> missed true positives.
>
> • Decontextualised Responses: A \"critical\" severity alert on an
> isolated lab machine may pose near-zero risk, whereas a \"medium\"
> alert on a core domain controller could signal a catastrophic breach.
>
> • \"Whack-a-Mole\" Reactivity: Focusing only on high-severity signals
> often results in reactive, tactical responses that fail to address the
> strategic attack lifecycle or the adversary\'s ultimate objectives.

**The Strategic Value of Risk-Based Decisions\**

A risk-driven approach aligns SOC activities with the organisation's
mission-critical goals:

> • Asset Criticality: Decisions are prioritised based on whether a
> threat targets \"crown jewels\"---assets that, if compromised, would
> shut down business operations or cause significant financial and
> reputational harm.
>
> • Intelligence-Led Defence: By using Cyber Threat Intelligence (CTI),
> the SOC can prioritise threats based on the Tactics, Techniques, and
> Procedures (TTPs) of actors known to target their specific industry or
> region, rather than generic software bugs.
>
> • Operational Continuity: The primary mission of the SOC is to allow
> the organisation to continue to operate safely even during an attack;
> risk-driven decisions ensure that containment actions do not cause
> more damage to the business than the threat itself.

Severity is a static label assigned by a detection engine, while risk
reflects real-world impact, likelihood, and context. SOC decisions must
be risk-driven because severity alone does not account for asset value,
attacker progression, user context, or business impact.

A mature SOC prioritizes what could hurt the business most, not what
looks loudest on a dashboard.\
\
In CrowdStrike Falcon, severity is intentionally not the final decision
point. Falcon provides risk context beyond severity, including:

- Host criticality (domain controller, production server, exec laptop)

- User context (privileged user, service account, standard user)

- Behavior chaining across detections

- Attack stage (early execution vs near impact)

- Environmental rarity (first-seen, anomalous behavior)
