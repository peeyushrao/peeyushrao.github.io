## What is the primary mission of a Security Operations Center (SOC)?

The primary mission of a **Security Operations Center (SOC)** is to **identify, investigate, and respond to security threats** so that the organization can continue to operate safely—even in the presence of active cyberattacks.

The SOC functions as a centralized **command‑and‑control hub** responsible for resisting digital attacks, minimizing the impact of successful compromises, and ensuring **business continuity**. Modern SOCs move beyond pure prevention to a proactive model of **prevention, detection, and response**.

Ultimately, the SOC’s mission is to reduce **attacker dwell time**—the period an adversary remains undetected—thereby limiting financial, operational, reputational, and data loss.

---

## Core Functions and Objectives

The SOC operates through a continuous, cyclical workflow designed to protect systems and data.

### Monitoring and Surveillance
- Continuous visibility across endpoints, networks, and applications
- Identification of unusual or potentially malicious activity through logs and telemetry

### Incident Triage
- Evaluation of alerts generated by security tools
- Differentiation between **true positives** (real threats) and **false positives** (noise)

### Investigation and Analysis
- Deep forensic analysis to determine:
  - What happened
  - When it happened
  - How it happened
  - The scope and origin of the intrusion

### Remediation and Recovery
- Containment of active threats
- Eradication of malicious artifacts (e.g., malware, persistence mechanisms)
- Restoration of systems to a known‑good state

---

## The Incident Lifecycle Responsibility

The SOC owns the **entire incident lifecycle**, including:

1. Continuous monitoring for anomalies
2. Alert triage and prioritization
3. Technical investigation and root‑cause analysis
4. Containment, eradication, and recovery

The mission is not merely to close alerts, but to **stop adversaries and restore secure operations** as quickly as possible.

---

Experienced interviewers expect the SOC mission to reflect the following principles:

### Operational Continuity

## What is the difference between an alert, a detection, and an incident?

Understanding the distinction between **alerts**, **detections**, and **incidents** is fundamental to effective SOC operations. These terms represent different levels of analysis and confidence in the security event lifecycle.

---

## High‑Level Definitions

- **Alert**: A raw signal indicating that a predefined condition or threshold was met.
- **Detection**: An alert that has been analyzed and classified as suspicious or malicious using logic, context, or analytics.
- **Incident**: A collection of one or more related detections that together represent a confirmed or likely security event requiring response.

In practice, SOCs may receive thousands of alerts daily, far fewer detections, and only a small number of incidents that warrant activation of an Incident Response Plan.

---

## Alert

An alert is the **earliest and noisiest** signal in the pipeline.

### Characteristics
- Generated automatically by a security control
- Triggered when a rule, threshold, or condition is met
- Lacks sufficient context to determine intent

### Example Triggers
- High volume of network traffic
- Failed login thresholds exceeded
- Process execution or network connection events

Alerts indicate **something happened**, not whether it is malicious.

---

## Detection

A detection is produced when alerts are enriched with **context, intelligence, or analytics** and identified as likely malicious.

### Characteristics
- Represents a security tool’s analytic decision
- Uses logic, behavior, or threat intelligence
- Provides confidence that activity aligns with attacker tactics

A detection answers the question: *Is this likely malicious behavior rather than noise?*

---

## Incident

An incident represents the **confirmation of an adverse security event**.

### Characteristics
- Composed of one or more related detections
- Indicates policy violation, compromise, or imminent threat
- Triggers coordinated response and containment actions

Incidents are what SOC Leads and Incident Response teams actively manage.

---

## Example Scenario: Failed Logins on a Critical Server

### 1. Alert
- The SIEM triggers an alert after **five failed login attempts within one minute**

### 2. Detection
- Analyst review shows:
  - Attempts targeting the `Administrator` account
  - Source IP is known malicious
- Classified as an **Initial Access** attempt

### 3. Incident
- One login attempt succeeds
- A new user account is created
- Persistence is achieved

At this stage, an **incident is declared** and response procedures are initiated.

---

In **CrowdStrike Falcon**, alerts, detections, and incidents are explicitly separated and handled differently.

---

## Alert (Event / Signal)

### SOC Reality
- Incidents—not alerts—drive response actions
- This is the level at which containment, eradication, and recovery occur

---

## Key Takeaway

- **Alerts** generate noise
- **Detections** generate confidence
- **Incidents** generate action

A mature SOC focuses on reducing alert fatigue, improving detection quality, and responding decisively to real incidents.

## Why is context more important than severity alone?

**Severity is a static label; context explains risk.**

Without context, severity can be misleading. A *low‑severity* alert may represent an active breach, while a *high‑severity* alert may be completely benign. Context provides the **big picture** needed to determine whether a signal represents real adversarial activity or simple digital noise.

Severity is typically a score based on potential impact. **Context**, however, answers the critical investigative questions: **who**, **where**, **how**, **what happened before**, and **what could happen next**. This is what allows analysts to identify intent and prioritize response effectively.

---

## 1. Differentiating Threats from Routine Activity

High‑severity alerts viewed in isolation are often false positives. Context is what separates routine operations from real threats.

### The "Normal" Baseline
- To recognize abnormal behavior, analysts must first understand what *normal* looks like in their environment
- Activities that appear dangerous in one organization may be standard in another

### Process Context
- Processes like `svchost.exe` are common on Windows systems
- Context such as **disk location**, **parent process**, and **execution chain** determines whether it is:
  - A legitimate system service
  - A malicious binary masquerading as a trusted process

Without this context, severity alone cannot reliably indicate risk.

---

## 2. Accurate Prioritization and Triage

Relying solely on severity often leads to **alert fatigue**, where analysts are overwhelmed by high‑severity noise and miss real threats.

### Situational Awareness
- Context helps determine:
  - Attacker dwell time
  - Current stage of the attack lifecycle
  - Whether activity is isolated or part of a broader campaign

### Resource Allocation
- Threat intelligence adds context about:
  - Which vulnerabilities are actively exploited
  - Which industries or environments are targeted

This allows teams to prioritize **medium‑severity risks under active exploitation** over **critical‑severity risks that are theoretical or inactive**.

---

## 3. Enabling an Actionable Response

Context transforms raw notifications into **actionable intelligence** by answering the investigative question: *so what?*

### Correlation
- Correlating timestamps and events across:
  - Endpoints
  - Firewalls
  - Proxies
  - Identity logs

This builds a timeline that reveals the **origin, progression, and scope** of an incident.

### Attribution and Intent
- Understanding adversary **Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures (TTPs)** provides context to:
  - Predict next steps
  - Identify lateral movement paths
  - Choose effective containment actions

Severity does not explain intent. Context does.

---

## CrowdStrike Falcon Perspective

In **CrowdStrike Falcon**, severity is only a **starting point**. Investigations are driven by contextual signals such as:

- Process ancestry (parent → child relationships)
- User identity (standard user vs admin vs service account)
- Host criticality (workstation, server, domain controller, executive laptop)
- Behavioral sequence (execution → credential access → lateral movement)
- Environmental baseline (first‑time behavior vs common activity)

---

## Example: Low Severity, High Risk

### Scenario

- Executed by `winword.exe`
- Encoded command present
- Followed by outbound network traffic

**Result:** High‑risk incident despite low severity

**Severity Says:** Low risk  
**Context Says:** Active compromise

---

## Key Takeaway

- **Severity prioritizes alerts**
- **Context prioritizes risk**

A mature SOC does not respond to alerts based on severity scores alone. It responds based on **contextual understanding of attacker behavior**, business impact, and attack progression.

## SOC decisions should be risk-driven, not severity-driven

SOC decisions must be **risk-driven** because risk combines technical severity with **business context, asset criticality, and the likelihood of adversary success**. While severity is a point-in-time score assigned by a security tool, **risk** represents the real-world calculation of **impact × probability** for a specific environment.

Severity answers *how bad could this be in theory?*  
Risk answers *how bad is this for **us**, right now?*

---

## Why Severity Alone Fails

Severity-driven prioritization creates operational blind spots and inefficiencies.

### Alert Fatigue
- Tool-defined severity often produces large volumes of "high-priority" noise
- Alerts may be technically severe but irrelevant to the organization
- Result: analyst burnout and missed true positives

### De-contextualized Responses
- A **critical** alert on an isolated lab system may pose near-zero risk
- A **medium** alert on a domain controller may represent an existential threat

Severity without context leads to **misaligned urgency**.

### "Whack-a-Mole" Reactivity
- Chasing high-severity alerts encourages tactical, short-term fixes
- Fails to address the attacker’s broader lifecycle or objectives

This results in reactive SOCs that respond to noise instead of adversaries.

---

## The Strategic Value of Risk-Based Decisions

A risk-driven SOC aligns security operations with **business outcomes**, not dashboards.

### Asset Criticality
- Prioritizes threats targeting **crown-jewel assets**
- Focuses on systems whose compromise would:
  - Halt operations
  - Cause financial loss
  - Damage reputation

### Intelligence-Led Defense
- Uses Cyber Threat Intelligence (CTI) to prioritize:
  - Actor-specific TTPs
  - Industry-targeted campaigns
  - Regional threat activity

This is more effective than reacting to generic vulnerabilities.

### Operational Continuity
- The SOC’s mission is to keep the business running—even during an attack
- Risk-driven decisions ensure containment actions do not cause more harm than the threat itself

---

## Severity vs Risk (Key Distinction)

- **Severity**: Static label assigned by a detection engine
- **Risk**: Dynamic evaluation of:
  - Asset value
  - Attacker progression
  - User context
  - Likelihood of success
  - Business impact

A mature SOC prioritizes **what could hurt the business most**, not what looks loudest on a dashboard.

---

## CrowdStrike Falcon Perspective

In **CrowdStrike Falcon**, severity is intentionally **not** the final decision point. Falcon provides rich risk context beyond severity, including:

- Host criticality (domain controller, production server, executive laptop)
- User context (privileged user, service account, standard user)
- Behavioral chaining across detections
- Attack stage (early execution vs near impact)
- Environmental rarity (first-seen or anomalous behavior)

Falcon investigations prioritize **risk escalation**, not severity escalation.

---

## Key Takeaway

- Severity tells you **what looks bad**
- Risk tells you **what matters**

**Effective SOCs are risk-driven.** They focus on protecting business-critical assets, disrupting attackers before impact, and making decisions that balance security with operational reality.

## What information should be gathered before escalating an alert?

Before escalating an alert, an analyst should gather **sufficient context** to determine whether the alert is a **true positive or false positive**, understand its **potential business impact**, and assess the **scope of activity**. Escalation should occur only after a preliminary investigation has converted a raw signal into an **actionable incident**.

---

## 1. Primary System and Host Information

The first step is establishing the **who** and **where** of the alert.

### Host Details
- Hostname
- IP address
- Operating system and version

### Asset Criticality
- Is the host a high‑value target?
  - Domain controller
  - Production server
  - System handling sensitive data (PII, financial records)

### System State
- System uptime
- Current resource utilization

This helps distinguish system issues from active attacks.

---

## 2. Process and Execution Context

Understanding what code is executing is essential for identifying malicious intent.

### Process Information
- Process name
- Process ID (PID)
- Parent process

### Command Line Arguments
- Full command line used for execution
- Indicators of living‑off‑the‑land abuse (e.g., PowerShell, CMD, MSHTA)

### File Integrity
- File path
- Hashes (MD5, SHA‑256)
- File creation and modification times

---

## 3. User Activity and Authentication

Attackers frequently abuse valid credentials, making user context critical.

### Account Details
- Username and domain
- Privilege level (standard user, administrator, service account)

### Login Patterns
- Multiple failed login attempts
- Unusual login times
- Logins from unfamiliar geographic locations

### Session Tracking
- Correlate activity using the Logon ID
- Identify all actions performed during the same session

---

## 4. Network and Communication Data

Network telemetry provides evidence of command‑and‑control (C2) or data exfiltration.

### Connection Metadata
- Source IP
- Destination IP
- Destination port

### Traffic Volume
- Sudden spikes in outbound data (possible exfiltration)
- Unexpected inbound traffic (payload delivery)

### Protocol Anomalies
- Non‑standard protocols over common ports (e.g., HTTPS abuse on port 443)
- Indicators of DNS tunneling or beaconing behavior

---

## 5. External Threat Intelligence

Validating internal findings against external intelligence improves confidence.

### Reputation Checks
- IP addresses
- Domains
- File hashes

### Attacker TTP Mapping
- Match observed behavior to known Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures (TTPs)
- Identify the attacker’s stage in the kill chain

---

## CrowdStrike Falcon Escalation Context

In **CrowdStrike Falcon**, escalation‑ready context typically includes the following dimensions.

---

## 1. Process Context

- Process name and path
- Command line
- Parent and grandparent processes
- Execution location (e.g., `System32` vs user‑writable paths)

**Falcon Data Sources:**
- `ProcessRollup2`
- Process Tree

---

## 2. User and Identity Context

- Logged‑in user
- Privilege level (standard user, admin, service account)
- Interactive vs non‑interactive session

Escalation urgency changes immediately if a **privileged account** is involved.

---

## 3. Host Context

- Host role (workstation, server, domain controller)
- Business criticality
- Internet‑facing status
- Previous detections on the same host

**Falcon Views:**
- Host Summary
- Host Timeline

---

## 4. Behavioral Sequence (Before and After)

Escalation should be driven by **behavior chains**, not isolated events.

### Key Questions
- What happened before the alert?
- What happened after the alert?

### Indicators to Review
- Network connections
- Credential access attempts
- Child process creation
- Persistence mechanisms

---

## 5. Rarity and Baseline

- First‑seen behavior?
- Rare process or DLL in the environment?
- Normal for this user or host?

Falcon’s historical and fleet‑wide baseline is critical for this assessment.

---

## 6. Scope Indicators

Determine whether activity is isolated or widespread.

- Same behavior on other hosts
- Same user across multiple systems
- Same hash or command observed elsewhere

---

## Key Takeaway

Alerts should be escalated **only when context confirms risk**.

- Events are signals
- Context reveals intent
- Behavior chains justify escalation

A mature SOC escalates **incidents**, not alerts.

## What is alert fatigue and why is it dangerous?

Building on the importance of **context** and **risk‑driven decision‑making**, alert fatigue emerges when those filters are missing or ineffective.

**Alert fatigue** occurs when security analysts are overwhelmed by the sheer volume of notifications generated by security tools—many of which are false positives or low‑value signals. The constant flood of alerts that must be collected, categorized, and interpreted degrades an analyst’s ability to identify what actually matters.

---

## Why Alert Fatigue Is Dangerous

Alert fatigue directly weakens an organization’s defensive capability.

### Missing Critical Threats
- High‑fidelity signals are buried under low‑value noise
- Analysts may overlook alerts that indicate active compromise

### Operational Errors
- Fatigued analysts are more likely to:
  - Close genuine incidents prematurely
  - Misinterpret logs or timelines
  - Miss important contextual clues

### Increased Attacker Dwell Time
- Critical indicators are delayed or ignored
- Attackers can remain undetected for weeks or months

### Analyst Burnout
- Repetitive, noisy work leads to exhaustion
- Results in high attrition and loss of institutional knowledge

### False Sense of Security
- Teams assume that because nothing critical was found yesterday, nothing is wrong today
- Excessive noise encourages complacency rather than vigilance

---

## Scenario: How Alert Fatigue Causes Real Breaches

### Initial Alert
- EDR tool triggers a **Medium‑severity** alert
- Single execution of `whoami.exe` on a financial server

### The Fatigue Failure
- Analyst has seen dozens of similar alerts caused by a legitimate IT auditing script
- Alert is closed without reviewing the **parent process**

### The Reality
- `whoami.exe` was spawned by a malicious Microsoft Word macro
- Execution originated from a weaponized document

### The Result
- Attacker escalates privileges
- Lateral movement occurs
- Initial access is missed because the signal was dismissed as noise

---

## CrowdStrike Falcon Perspective

In **CrowdStrike Falcon**, alert fatigue is explicitly addressed by design.

### How Falcon Reduces Alert Fatigue
- Separation of **raw events** from **detections**
- Correlation of detections into **incidents**
- Prioritization of **behavior chains**, not isolated events
- Use of **Indicators of Attack (IOAs)** and **machine learning**, not raw signatures

### Where Fatigue Can Still Appear
- Overly broad or poorly scoped custom IOAs
- Weak or noisy correlation rules
- Treating every detection as equal risk

---

## What Interviewers Want to Hear

> **"Alert fatigue is a detection engineering failure, not an analyst failure."**

The problem is rarely a lack of tools—it is a lack of **signal quality, prioritization, and context**.

---

## Key Takeaway

- Alerts are unavoidable
- Fatigue is optional

A mature SOC reduces alert fatigue by **engineering better detections**, prioritizing **risk and behavior**, and ensuring analysts focus on **meaningful signals—not noise**.

## What is a false positive and how do you confirm one?

A **false positive** is an alert or detection that incorrectly identifies **benign or authorized activity** as malicious. This typically occurs when security tools such as IDS, EDR, or SIEM platforms flag activity that matches a rule, signature, or analytic pattern but **lacks malicious intent**.

False positives are a major operational burden because they consume analyst time and resources that should be focused on **true incidents**.

---

## What a False Positive Is — and Is Not

- A false positive is a **detection that looks suspicious** but is ultimately **legitimate activity**
- A detection is **never a false positive by default**
- A detection becomes a false positive **only after investigation**

You confirm a false positive by validating **intent and legitimacy through context**, not by assuming safety based on familiarity or severity.

---

## How to Confirm a False Positive

Confirming a false positive requires a **systematic validation process**, not a quick dismissal.

---

## 1. Analyze Context and Severity

- Review alert metadata and triggering logic
- Determine whether activity aligns with:
  - Scheduled backups
  - Software updates
  - Administrative scripts
  - Approved automation

Severity alone is insufficient—context determines meaning.

---

## 2. Stakeholder Communication

- Consult relevant teams (IT, network, system administrators)
- Validate whether:
  - The activity was authorized
  - The timing matches planned changes

Stakeholder confirmation supports—but does not replace—technical validation.

---

## 3. Threat Intelligence Cross-Referencing

- Check reputation of involved artifacts:
  - File hashes
  - IP addresses
  - Domains and URLs

If multiple reputable intelligence sources classify artifacts as clean or trusted, the alert is **more likely** a false positive.

---

## 4. Behavioral Analysis (Sandboxing)

- Execute suspicious files or scripts in an isolated sandbox
- Observe runtime behavior

Indicators of malicious intent include:
- Persistence mechanisms
- Credential access
- Lateral movement
- Data exfiltration

Absence of these behaviors supports a false positive determination.

---

## 5. Frequency Analysis (Stacking)

- Determine how common the behavior is across the environment
- If the same process runs on hundreds or thousands of identical systems, it may be part of a **standard gold build**

Rare behavior is suspicious; common behavior is often legitimate.

---

## CrowdStrike Falcon Perspective

In **CrowdStrike Falcon**, confirming a false positive is a **structured investigation**, not a quick dismissal.

---

## Step 1: Validate the Behavior (Not the Tool)

- Review the process tree
- Inspect the full command line
- Identify parent and grandparent processes
- Look for behavior chaining across detections

---

## Step 2: Validate User and Host Context

- **Who executed it?**
  - Standard user
  - Administrator
  - Service account
- **Where did it run?**
  - Finance workstation
  - IT admin server
- **Is this expected for this user on this host?**

Falcon Host Summary and user context are critical here.

---

## Step 3: Check Historical Baseline

- Has this behavior occurred before?
- Is it common across the environment?
- Is this a first-seen event?

Falcon’s historical telemetry often determines **false positive vs true positive**.

---

## Step 4: Look for After-Effects

True false positives usually have **no follow-on activity**.

Indicators typically absent in false positives:
- Network beaconing
- Credential access attempts
- Persistence mechanisms
- Lateral movement

Malicious intent usually reveals itself **after execution**.

---

## Step 5: Validate the Business Use Case

- Is the tool approved?
- Is the command consistent with documented administrative workflows?
- Confirm with IT **only after** technical validation

> Never accept “IT does this” without evidence.

---

## Key Takeaway

- False positives are identified **through investigation**, not assumption
- Context, behavior, and baseline matter more than alert labels

A mature SOC confirms false positives carefully—protecting analysts from noise **without blinding the organization to real threats**.

## What is a true positive with low business risk?

A **true positive with low business risk** is a security event that correctly identifies **real malicious or policy‑violating activity**, but where the **potential impact to the organization is minimal**. The threat is genuine, yet the affected asset, data, or conditions significantly limit business harm.

This commonly occurs when verified malicious activity targets **low‑criticality systems**, such as isolated lab machines, test environments, or non‑production workstations that do not contain sensitive or mission‑critical data.

**In simple terms:**
> The threat is real, but the risk to the business is low.

---

## Why True Positive ≠ High Risk

Not every confirmed attack requires emergency response. Risk is determined not just by **malicious intent**, but by:
- What was targeted
- What data was exposed
- How far the attacker progressed
- Whether controls successfully limited impact

A mature SOC distinguishes between **detection accuracy** and **business impact**.

---

## Key Characteristics of Low‑Risk True Positives

Analysts classify an event as a low‑risk true positive by evaluating environmental and contextual factors.

### Asset Criticality
- The affected system is not essential to business operations
- Loss of the system would not cause:
  - Financial harm
  - Operational outage
  - Reputational damage

### Containment and Isolation
- The asset resides in a segmented or restricted network
- Lateral movement to high‑value systems is prevented

### Data Sensitivity
- No storage or processing of:
  - Personally Identifiable Information (PII)
  - Financial data
  - Intellectual property

### Attacker Sophistication
- Activity originates from:
  - Automated tooling
  - Opportunistic scanning
  - Low‑skill attackers (e.g., script kiddies)

Rather than targeted campaigns or advanced persistent threats (APTs).

---

## Alternative Definition (Operational View)

A true positive with low business risk is malicious or suspicious activity that:
- Is correctly identified
- Is contained early
- Targets low‑value assets
- Fails to progress through the attack lifecycle

Strong preventive and detective controls reduce impact even when detection confirms malicious behavior.

---

## CrowdStrike Falcon Perspective

In **CrowdStrike Falcon**, low‑risk true positives are common and must be handled **without overreaction**.

### Typical Examples

- Malware execution involving:
  - A non‑privileged user
  - A non‑critical workstation

### Falcon Response Outcome

- Falcon detects execution
- Malicious process is blocked or terminated
- Persistence is prevented
- Lateral movement does not occur

The threat is real, but Falcon’s controls successfully limit impact.

---

## SOC Handling Guidance

Low‑risk true positives should:
- Be documented and tracked
- Inform detection tuning and metrics
- Not trigger disruptive containment actions

They provide **valuable signal** without requiring high‑urgency response.

---

## Key Takeaway

- **True positive** confirms malicious activity
- **Low business risk** reflects limited impact

A mature SOC responds proportionally—acknowledging real threats while focusing effort on events that **materially endanger the business**.

## Why are accurate timestamps critical during investigations?

Accurate timestamps are the **chronological backbone** of any cybersecurity investigation. They allow analysts to determine **when** events occurred, reconstruct the **sequence of activity**, and correlate logs across disparate systems to build a **coherent narrative** of an intrusion.

Incident response is fundamentally a **timeline problem**. Without reliable timestamps, investigators cannot confidently determine what happened first, what caused what, how far an attacker progressed, or whether events are related at all.

---

## The Role of Timestamps in Analysis

### Reconstruction and Attribution
- Place events in precise temporal order
- Answer critical questions about **who** acted and **how** activity unfolded
- Establish causality between actions (e.g., execution → credential access)

### Log Correlation
- Link events across heterogeneous sources, such as:
  - Firewall connection logs
  - Endpoint process executions
  - Identity and authentication logs

Accurate time alignment enables analysts to match a network connection to the exact process and user that initiated it.

### Identifying Anomalies and Evasion
- Detect **timestomping**, where attackers alter file timestamps to blend in
- Identify inconsistencies in file metadata that suggest manipulation

### Measuring Attacker Progression
- Calculate **dwell time** (how long an attacker remained undetected)
- Measure **breakout time** (how quickly lateral movement occurred)

These metrics are impossible to calculate accurately without reliable timestamps.

---

## Forensic Validation Use Cases

Beyond simple correlation, timestamps are essential for validating forensic artifacts.

### File System Analysis (NTFS Example)
- If a file’s **Modified** time predates its **Created** time:
  - The file was likely copied onto the system
  - It is unlikely to be native to the host

### Timestomping Detection
- Manipulated timestamps are a strong indicator of **human adversary activity**
- Used to obscure intrusion timelines and evade investigation

Accurate timestamps allow defenders to identify and challenge these deception techniques.

---

## Consequences of Inaccurate Timestamps

Poor or inconsistent timestamps lead to:
- Incorrect timelines
- Missed scope and affected assets
- False assumptions about causality
- Wrong containment or remediation decisions

**Bad timestamps produce bad conclusions.**

## CrowdStrike Falcon Perspective

In **CrowdStrike Falcon**, investigations rely heavily on time‑based correlation across:

- Host Timeline
- Process execution events
- Detection sequences
- Incident graphs
- Cross‑host activity

Accurate timestamps enable analysts to:
- Reconstruct parent → child process execution correctly
- Identify pre‑ and post‑execution behavior
- Determine attacker dwell time
- Correlate activity across hosts and users
- Align endpoint activity with identity, network, and cloud telemetry

---

## Why Order Matters: Example

- **Credential access detected *after* execution**
  - Strong indicator of privilege escalation

- **Credential access detected *before* execution**
  - Often benign or system‑initiated behavior

Without accurate timestamps, this distinction collapses—and so does investigative confidence.

---

## Key Takeaway

- Investigations succeed or fail on timelines
- Timestamps provide causality, scope, and confidence

A mature SOC treats time synchronization and timestamp accuracy as **foundational controls**, not implementation details.
